Using Boeing's 2024 10-K, a 3-point rise in the interest rate on all of its debt would have made 2024's net loss about **$1.56 billion bigger, roughly 13% worse**. Boeing lost money in 2024, so the extra interest deepens a loss rather than cutting a profit.

**Inputs (FY2024, $ millions)**
- Debt (the XBRL `LongTermDebt` figure at 12/31/2024): $53,625. I'm treating this as all of Boeing's debt; I didn't check whether it includes the current portion.
- Interest and debt expense: $2,725. Divided by the debt, that's an average rate of about 5.1%, so the scenario puts it at about 8.1%.
- Loss before income taxes: $(12,210)
- Income tax benefit: $381, an effective rate of about 3.1%
- Net loss attributable to Boeing shareholders: $(11,817)

**Calculation**
- Extra interest before tax: $53,625 × 3% = **$1,609**, which would raise interest expense by about 59%, from $2,725 to about $4,334.
- After tax at the 3.1% effective rate: $1,609 × (1 − 0.031) ≈ **$1,559**.
- New net loss: about **$(13,376)**, compared with $(11,817).
- Change: $1,559 ÷ $11,817 ≈ **13.2% worse**.

**How the tax assumption changes the result**
| Tax assumption | Net income impact | % change |
|---|---|---|
| No tax shield | −$1,609M | −13.6% |
| 3.1% effective rate (base case) | −$1,559M | −13.2% |
| 21% US statutory rate | −$1,271M | −10.8% |

I used the effective rate as the base case because Boeing's tax benefit was very small next to its pre-tax loss. The 10-K's share-based compensation table notes figures "before consideration of valuation allowance," which suggests a valuation allowance on deferred tax assets limits its tax benefits. That makes the 21% case the least likely.

**Assumptions:** the higher rate applies to the whole year-end 2024 balance for a full year, and nothing else changes (no capitalized interest, hedges or effect on operations).

**Sources**
- Boeing 10-K for FY2024, filed 2025-02-03, accession 0000012927-25-000015: income statement and XBRL figures for `NetIncomeLoss` and `LongTermDebt`.
